# The Soul of boxlore

Technology was never supposed to be a walled garden.

The original promise of personal computing, and Android in particular, was simple: you bought the hardware, so you owned the device. You had the freedom to choose your tools, install whatever software you wanted, and explore without having to ask a gatekeeper for permission. 

It is surreal to watch that freedom slip away. There is a wild audacity in Google taking an operating system built on the back of open source and slowly turning it into the very walled garden it was created to replace. When a platform chokes sideloading, treats independent APKs with suspicion, and tries to gatekeep what software you can run on hardware you already paid for, it isn't protecting users; it is turning a personal computer into a leased terminal. That is why [keeping Android open](https://keepandroidopen.org/) matters so much.

The apps themselves followed the exact same path. We don't blame companies for trying to make money; running servers is expensive and everyone has bills to pay. But in the race to monetize every tap, simple tools got turned into crowded obstacle courses: paywalls for basic playback features, clean screens cluttered with promotional noise, and artificial barriers placed between people and the things they want to enjoy.

Podcasting is one of the rare places that remained open. Built on decentralized RSS and preserved by independent communities like the [Podcast Index](https://podcastindex.org/), no single corporation owns it or gets to decide what you are allowed to hear. 

We already saw what happened when giants tried to privatize it. Spotify poured over a billion dollars into buying up studios and exclusive paywalls, while Google killed its dedicated player to herd listeners into the YouTube algorithm. But closed silos keep failing against open standards. Audiences refused to be enclosed, creators watched their cultural reach collapse, and exclusive shows quietly surrendered back to open RSS feeds. It was a rare, decisive victory for the open web: proof that a decentralized standard could outlast a corporate land grab.

We built **boxlore** to be an app worthy of that openness: fast, quiet, and built with care. We spend late nights thinking through every way an edge case could break, understanding how someone expects a control to react, and fighting to shave 0.1 seconds off an API response.

None of this exists in a vacuum. boxlore is possible only because we are standing on the shoulders of open source developers and open protocol advocates: the open RSS standard that has kept podcasting permissionless for twenty years, the builders of Android Media3, ExoPlayer, Room, SQLite, and the broader Kotlin ecosystem. We are building on tools that other craftspeople shared with the world.

That is why boxlore has no ads, no subscriptions, and no paywalls. It is free, and it will stay free, because software that helps people learn, listen, and explore should simply exist without a catch.

Technology shouldn't control your world. It just needs to give you the tools, step aside, and let you define it yourself.
